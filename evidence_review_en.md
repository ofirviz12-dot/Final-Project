# Evidence Review and Existing Solutions

**Project:** Detection of images and videos generated or modified using AI  
**Research planning document:** November 2026 | **Scope:** Images and videos, including localized AI editing; excludes text detection  
**Document type:** Evidence Review and Alternatives | **Status:** Preliminary design decisions; project experiments have not yet been conducted

## 1. Purpose, Scope, and Decision Question

The aim of this project is to build a system that helps users assess whether an image or video shows signs of having been **generated or modified** using artificial intelligence. The system is primarily intended for checking suspicious content on social media; however, it does not determine whether an event actually occurred or whether an accompanying claim is true.

**The detection scope includes three distinct scenarios:** (1) an image or video frames that were generated entirely by AI (*AI-Generated*); (2) a genuine photograph **locally edited** using AI, for example by adding, removing, or replacing an object through *inpainting* (*AI-Modified*); and (3) facial or video manipulation such as a *deepfake*. A model that detects one scenario cannot be assumed to work for the others [1, 3, 12, 13].

**Central decision question:** How should we design a practical, accessible, and cautious solution that provides useful results, also checks for localized edits, and enables us to measure a technical contribution beyond using a single detector or an existing detection tool?

## 2. Search Strategy and Source Selection

The review focuses on five types of evidence: (a) research on image detection, generalization, and robustness; (b) research on detecting and **localizing** AI-based edits; (c) papers and datasets concerning video manipulation; (d) existing consumer and research solutions; and (e) standards and official documentation. Primary sources were selected: conference and journal publications, academic project sites, official product pages, and technical specifications.

Search terms included `AI-generated image detection`, `unseen generators`, `image manipulation localization`, `diffusion inpainting detection`, `text-guided inpainting benchmark`, `deepfake benchmark`, `in-the-wild detection`, `video frame sampling`, `AI detector browser extension`, and `content provenance`. For every source, we considered relevance to intended users, input type, output type (a whole-image score versus a pixel-level mask), limitations, implementation availability, and implications for our decisions. Vendor marketing claims about performance are not independent evidence of experimental success. **Reference and bibliographic details last checked: November 21, 2026.** The NeurIPS [2, 4], Springer [13], and CVF [1, 3] PDF links were verified against official publication pages or the papers themselves. For CVF PDF links [5, 12], their URL patterns and publication records were checked, but automated direct access returned HTTP 403; alternative PDF locations are provided. The interactive model page [9] was also cross-checked against a university publication.

## 3. Findings by Theme

### 3.1 Generalization to New Generators and Media Quality

Ojha, Li, and Lee [1] investigated generalization to generators not encountered during training. They used features extracted from a pretrained `CLIP ViT-L/14`, without retraining the visual representation specifically for fake-image detection, and compared `nearest neighbor` with `linear probing`. Their comparisons also examined representations from `CLIP ResNet-50`, `ImageNet ResNet-50`, and `ImageNet ViT-B/16`. The findings support evaluating different representations and unseen generators, rather than choosing a detector solely on reported accuracy. GenImage [2] additionally provides `cross-generator` and degraded-image evaluation tasks involving blurred, compressed, or low-resolution images. **Implication:** We will select detectors and test them on sources not used for calibration, including processing conditions resembling social-media sharing.

### 3.2 Facial Manipulation and Fair Video Evaluation

FaceForensics++ [3] includes 1,000 original videos and four manipulation methods: `DeepFakes`, `Face2Face`, `FaceSwap`, and `NeuralTextures`. The downloadable dataset provides the compression levels `raw/c0` (no lossy compression), `c23` (light H.264 compression), and `c40` (heavy compression), as well as masks for manipulated regions. DeepfakeBench [4] provides a common evaluation framework covering 15 methods across 9 datasets, with standardized input, processing, and metrics. Deepfake-Eval-2024 [5] contains media circulated during 2024; **the conference paper appeared at CVPR Workshops 2026** (with an earlier arXiv version in 2025). The work highlights the generalization gap between laboratory benchmarks and real-world media. Accordingly, we will assess images and videos separately and regard frame-level classification as a baseline only: it cannot guarantee detection of every brief, motion-related, or facial modification.

### 3.3 Detecting Localized Edits: AI-Modified / Inpainting

**TruFor** [12] proposes an *Image Forgery Detection and Localization* approach that fuses RGB visual information with `Noiseprint++`—a representation sensitive to noise and manipulation traces—through a Transformer-based fusion mechanism. Unlike a whole-image classifier, the system produces a **pixel-level localization map**, a **confidence map**, and an image-level integrity score. The study offers a general manipulation-detection framework, **but it does not establish reliable detection of every new diffusion-based edit**.

**TGIF2** [13], a 2026 study of *text-guided inpainting*, specifically evaluates localized image edits made with `Stable Diffusion 2`, `SDXL`, and `FLUX.1`. It distinguishes **Spliced** images—in which a generated region is inserted into the original image—from **Fully Regenerated** images—in which the image is regenerated even though the intended edit concerns a specific region; it also compares semantic and random masks. The study reports that localization detectors, including TruFor, struggle with Fully Regenerated cases and with generalization to some FLUX.1 variants. Its experiments also suggest possible dependence on object boundaries. Consequently, **a whole-image AI score is not a substitute for locating an edited region**, and a heatmap does not constitute definitive proof [12, 13].

**Required architectural implication:** Alongside the global-classification pipeline, the system will have a separate *localization* pipeline that receives an image and returns a `tamper mask`, a confidence score, and an indication of possible localized editing. A decision layer will combine outputs **without erasing the distinction** between full generation and local modification. When supported by the evidence, the report will display the suspected manipulation type and regions, together with an *Inconclusive* option. If no localization model performs adequately on relevant inpainting examples, we will not present localized-edit detection as a solved capability; instead, we will conduct a focused experiment and consider a different model or limited adaptation.

### 3.4 Existing Solutions and Implications for Novelty

BitMind [6] offers a browser extension for identifying AI content while browsing; AI or Not [7] provides checks for images and other media inputs; F-Secure [8] supports uploaded images and videos; and DeepFake-O-Meter [9] is a research-oriented platform that brings together several detectors. Therefore, an upload website, a browser extension, or simply running multiple detectors does not constitute sufficient novelty by itself. The contribution to be evaluated is a **clearly defined and measurable fusion method** for whole-image generation detectors, localized-edit detectors, and video detectors, including handling disagreement, abstention, and computational cost. We will not claim superior performance in advance or assert that independently untested products lack particular features.

### 3.5 Provenance Evidence and Limits of Inference

The `C2PA Technical Specification`, **version 2.4 (April 2026)** [10], and the Content Authenticity Initiative's open verification software [11] allow verification of signed *Content Credentials* when present. This is a complementary evidence channel, **separate** from detector output: absent credentials do not prove forgery, and valid credentials do not establish the factual truth of the content. Provenance verification may be incorporated into the system, but missing metadata must not be treated as positive evidence of AI generation.

## 5. Synthesis: Conclusions and Preliminary Decisions

The research suggests that the central challenge is **reliability under changing conditions**, not merely a high score on a single dataset. The products show that user-facing checking workflows and multi-detector platforms already exist. Moreover, TruFor and TGIF2 show that distinguishing **global detection** from **localized-edit detection** is necessary to cover the project's intended scope [12, 13]. Taken together, the sources point toward a solution centered on experimental comparison of methods, transparent communication of uncertainty, and an interface that does not present a probability as proof.

- **Proposed MVP:** A website where users upload an image or video, run existing models, and receive one of three decision outcomes: **“Signs of AI generation/modification detected,” “Insufficient signs detected,”** or **“Inconclusive.”** Where evidence supports it, the result will also specify the suspected type—full generation, localized modification, or facial manipulation. The second outcome must not be interpreted as “definitely real.”
- **Algorithmic research:** Compare a single detector, a simple mean, fixed weights, and fusion affected by media quality/detector agreement. Thresholds and calibration will be established using validation data only; localization outputs will not be converted into equivalent global scores without evaluation.
- **Video:** Begin with uniform frame sampling and score aggregation; compare scene-change/quality-aware selection at the same frame budget, and consider a dedicated facial-manipulation detector later if needed.
- **Interface:** Show individual model outputs, masks/suspected regions when available, processing time, limitations, and a warning against treating the result as conclusive proof. An Android floating button will remain an extension rather than a dependency of the MVP.

### 5.1 System Alternatives and Preferred Direction

A locally run engine using open models is our preferred research baseline because it supports reproducible experiments, control over data processing, and less reliance on commercial API quotas. API services may be used for limited comparisons, subject to terms of use and budget. The **global pipeline** will produce an image-level score; the **localization pipeline** will produce regional suspicion, a mask, and confidence; and the **video pipeline** will run relevant pipelines on selected frames and aggregate evidence at the clip level. A **decision and presentation layer** will preserve the difference between full generation and localized editing and permit abstention. C2PA verification, if included, will be displayed separately. These are proposed components; their practical performance has not yet been demonstrated.

### 5.2 Exit Check — Which Decisions Changed Because of the Evidence?

**Revised starting assumptions:** (a) The assumption that binary classification of an entire file is sufficient has been replaced with **three decision outcomes, including “Inconclusive,”** in response to the generalization gaps documented by Ojha and Deepfake-Eval-2024 [1, 5]. This is a **proposed design response**, not proof that abstention has already improved accuracy. (b) The assumption that a global classifier would also cover partial edits has been replaced with a **separate localization pipeline**, based on TruFor's localization/confidence outputs and the challenges documented by TGIF2 [12, 13]. (c) The possibility of starting with an Android floating button has been moved to an **extension phase**, while a web application is selected for the MVP: existing tools already provide checking workflows [6, 8, 9], whereas `MediaProjection` involves permission and repeated consent requirements [14]. The preference for the web is **our assessment of scope and resources**, not a comparative experiment showing that the web is inherently better.

**What evidence would make us change direction?** If a localization detector fails to meet predefined localization metrics on `Spliced` and `Fully Regenerated` edits, especially with random masks [13], we will test an alternative localization model, limited adaptation, or narrower support claims. If detector fusion increases the false-positive rate or does not outperform a single model [1, 4, 5], we will retain the single detector as a baseline and revise the fusion rule. If frame sampling misses short modifications [3, 4], we will increase sampling, use adaptive selection, or add a dedicated video detector. If local inference exceeds time or memory budgets, we will first reduce the number of models before introducing reliance on an API. **For every change, we will record the source, measurable finding, decision, and rationale**, consistent with the course presentation's exit-check question.

## 6. Evaluation Plan, Limitations, and Open Questions

- **File-level detection:** `Precision`, `Recall`, `F1`, `ROC-AUC`, false-positive rate, processing time, calibration, and coverage when “Inconclusive” is permitted. Where data allow, results will be reported separately for fully generated media, localized editing, and facial deepfakes.
- **Localized detection:** `pixel-level F1` and mask `IoU`; image-level detection metrics; comparisons of `Spliced` versus `Fully Regenerated` and `Semantic` versus `Random` masks [12, 13]. A localization metric must not be replaced by image-classification ROC-AUC.
- **Fairness and robustness:** Strict separation of training/validation/test data; duplicate prevention; no frames from the same source video across splits; held-out generators; JPEG, resizing, and blur tests; and equal time/frame budgets for alternatives [1–5].
- **Alternatives:** Compare a single detector, an unweighted mean, and proposed fusion; uniform versus adaptive video sampling; and the additional value of a localization pipeline versus global classification alone.

Risks and unresolved questions include licenses and availability of model weights and datasets, required computing resources, alignment of ground-truth masks with edited regions, false positives, abstention thresholds, failures on new editing methods, and privacy of user-uploaded media. **This document presents evidence and design implications—not results from experiments already carried out in this project.**
